# 03 — Deductible Expenses

*The part everybody secretly came for. And the part with the most rules-are-
nuanced, check-with-a-pro energy. We'll give you the map, the vocabulary, and
a printable checklist — not legal nitroglycerin.*

## The golden rule

An expense is potentially deductible if it's:

1. **Ordinary** — common in your line of work (a writer buying editing? yes)
2. **Necessary** — appropriate and helpful for the business (not necessarily
   "required," just sensible)
3. **Documented** — you have the receipt and a one-line reason

Money spent for the business, from the business account, for a business
purpose: ba-dump. That's 95% of the intuition. Chapter 05's system keeps #3
painless.

## What authors and small biz commonly deduct

| Category | Examples (author / small-biz flavored) | Gotcha |
|---|---|---|
| **Advertising & marketing** | Book promo sites, social ads, business cards, launch swag | Only business-purpose ads; skip anything political |
| **Editing, cover art, formatting** | Editors, illustrators, Vellum-style software, typesetting | Freelancers you pay $600+/yr may need a 1099-NEC from *you* — see 06 or the handbook |
| **Website & tech** | Hosting, domain, email list tool, subscriptions, AI tools for the *business* | Don't deduct purely personal Netflix-style accounts |
| **Office supplies** | Paper, ink, a keyboard, a chair | "Ordinary & necessary," not a shopping spree |
| **Hardware & software** | Laptop, better mic for audiobooks, office suite | Big-ticket items may need to be deducted over years (depreciation) or via a special election — pro territory |
| **Home office** | A slice of rent/mortgage interest, utilities, internet, i.e., the % of home used *exclusively & regularly* for business | Must be a real designated space; guest bed with a laptop on it fails "exclusive" |
| **Vehicle mileage** | Book fair, picking up printer-paper pallets, meeting a collaborator | Standard vs. actual method; current rates live on IRS.gov, not in this guide |
| **Education & research** | Craft books, courses related to the business, market-research purchases (yes, "I bought three chess books" can be *research* if you write chess content) | Not a public library replacement; keep it real |
| **Professional services** | CPA, lawyer, tax-prep software, LLC filing fees | Books about business ≠ deductible just because you wrote a book |

## Costs of goods and inventory

**Resellers and print-run people:** goods you buy *to sell* are **Cost of
Goods Sold (COGS)**, not "supplies." On Schedule C these live in a specific
section, separate from operating expenses. Track: beginning inventory, what
you bought, what's left at year-end, and what got sold.

- Thrift-flippers: what you paid for the item IS your COGS. Log it.
- Print-based folks: printing per unit = COGS; the order minimums/fees that
  bury real costs = often COGS too; book-signing pizza = not.
- Consignment/storage for inventory = ordinary business expense.

## The classic "maybe" list (a.k.a. — check with a pro)

- **Phone** — business-use % of a plan; honest estimation).
- **Meals** — business meals with a purpose/partner differ wildly from "I ate
  lunch at my desk"; rules around deductibility have shifted in recent years,
  so don't trust 2019-era TikToks here.
- **Travel** — attending a *specific* conference or research trip for a book
  can qualify; a vacation with a laptop in the luggage usually doesn't.
- **"I'm launching a book"** events — nuggets within this often *are*
  deductible; the blended ones (party/conference/personal) are exactly where
  you allocate and let a pro check the math.
- **Health insurance** — self-employed health premiums can sometimes reduce
  taxes in a special way, with income caps and melody of conditions. Strong
  pro-conversation candidate.

### What is *never* deductible

- **Personal** expenses relabeled in your head. That's the "don't do this"
  bin, and it's a waterfall, not a cliff. If your *intent* to define the
  expense as business-use was honest, your documentation will carry you.
- Capital stuff, office space you also let a kid nap in, the noticer of the
  whole thing, home-office space that isn't exclusive, your pet, your cable
  TV "for background," (mostly) your commute to a W-2 job's usual place.

## The home-office slice, in one diagram

Let's say your spare room's 120 sq ft out of a 1,200 sq ft home = **10%**.
Ten percent of qualifying home costs is a candidate deduction, *if* that room
is used regularly and exclusively for the business. Deviation minor, kid
napping there = disqualifying. There's also a simplified square-footage
method with a rate that changes — current figure on IRS.gov, not here.

## How to *record* them calmly

One row per expense, in the same humble spreadsheet/bookkeeping app:

```
date | vendor | what | why | amount | category | receipt-file-name
```

The `why` column is gold: *"author headshots for website"* won't read as vague
to future you — or to any future reviewer.

## Call a pro when...

- You're buying big obvious capital equipment (laptop, printer, vehicle) —
  depreciation vs. expensing elections are person-specific.
- You want to mix business/personal numbers (home office %, phone %, vehicle
  use logs) — your pro will pick the reasonable method.
- You want to know whether to **elect** expensing (bonus/Section-179 names are
  enough to memorize) this year vs. next, based on *your* projected profits.
- Your receipts live in three apps, a shoebox, and your camera roll and you're
  afraid. (Fix the fear with Chapter 05, then book the pro.)
