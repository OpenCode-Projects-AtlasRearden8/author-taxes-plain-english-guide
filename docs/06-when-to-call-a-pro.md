# 06 — When to Call a Pro

*The chapter that keeps the previous five honest.*

## The short version

This manual is education. A **tax professional** (CPA, Enrolled Agent, or tax
attorney) is advice. You cross the line whenever:

- A decision is *yours to make about your specific numbers* — formations,
  elections, salary vs. draw, expenses on the gray-ish list
- Amounts are *large* relative to your life — "large" is defined by your gut,
  not the IRS
- Anything involving **penalties, audits, back-year filings, or back-tax
  cleanup** — professionals have tools and standing to fix these where
  guessing makes them worse
- You simply want a second brain. That's not weakness; that's *spending a few
  hundred dollars to protect a five-figure liability*. Excellent ROI.

## Your tripwire list (skimmable, bookmarkable)

- First year with any real Schedule C profit
- Year one of an LLC — especially choosing taxation (ignored vs. S/C corp)
- Quarterly estimates: you're unsure what to send or missed a date
- You **hire** anyone, even a $700 editor you paid by PayPal ($600+ per year
  generally means you owe them a 1099 — a pro/file helps)
- Big purchases (vehicle, laptop stack, equipment) — you want depreciation
  elections done *right*, not approximately
- You're mixing personal money in the business account (clean-up help)
- Multi-state sales, foreign platform income, any state-country taxes due
- Anything arrives from IRS or a state with a scary letterhead — **never** respond
  to emails/texts claiming to be IRS first; verify independently
- You want to retire / wind down the business, sell a book's "list," or
  transfer assets — the exit is taxier than the entry

## What a pro costs and what you get (fair expectations)

- A consult with a CPA/EA often runs from a couple hundred dollars for a
  one-hour planning hour to a few hundred to your first preparation plus
  planning. Every experience differs; those numbers are vibe-level, not
  quotes.
- You buy outcomes: correct elections, penalty avoidance, a numbered list of
  exactly what to do next, and *protection* — if something's ever questioned,
  the file a pro built is much stronger.
- Some pros specialize in authors/creators/small biz. **Ask a pro before
  hiring them**: "Do you work with self-employed authors/creators?" A good
  one will happily spend five minutes matching fit.

## What to bring to a first meeting

1. Last year's tax return (all pages, including schedules)
2. This year's income log (Ch. 02 spreadsheet — deposits *and* platform
   statements if they differ)
3. Expense log and receipts — organized or even entirely raw; pros have seen
   worse
4. LLC papers: Articles, EIN letter, operating agreement if you have one
5. Any 1099s that have arrived (and the list of platforms that should have
   sent some)
6. Proof of estimated payments you've made (Ch. 04 folder)
7. Your questions, written down. (Seriously. Write them down. Brain-dumps in
   the car afterward are a tax on everyone.)

## Questions that make you sound like you did the reading

- "My business is a single-member LLC in Indiana; is it filing as disregarded
  on my 1040, or should I discuss an election?"
- "Can you help me set up my quarterly estimate method for next year based on
  *this* return's numbers?"
- "Here's my home-office measurement — should I use actual or simplified?"
- "What documentation style do you want from me so your fee stays low?"
- "What records should I keep, and how long, for *my* situation?"

## The promise this book keeps

Every chapter pointed here. Not as a disclaimer pushed by lawyers — as the
honest architecture of being a self-employed person: **you get educated, you
keep calm records, and a pro validates the decisions that matter.** That
system costs less than one penalty and is infinitely kinder to Future-You.

Welcome to being a business owner. You've got this — and now you've also got
the receipts.
