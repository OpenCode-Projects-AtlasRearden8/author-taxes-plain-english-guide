# 01 — Schedule C in Plain English

*One form. It's shorter than your book. Let's read it like a map.*

## What Schedule C actually is

Schedule C ("Profit or Loss From Business") is a **one-page add-on** to your
normal Form 1040. It exists so sole proprietors and single-member LLCs can
tell the IRS: *here's what my business earned, here's what it cost, here's the
profit — tax me on that.*

Key idea: **the IRS taxes profit, not revenue.** If your books made $8,000 and
legitimately cost you $3,000 to produce, market, and run, you're taxed closer
to the $5,000 — not the full $8,000. Expenses are not a loophole; they're how
the math is supposed to work.

> Form numbers change names rarely; *rules and thresholds change yearly.* This
> chapter teaches the **map**, not today's coordinates. Confirm specifics at
> [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov) or with a tax professional.

## Who files one

- Sole proprietors (you anywhere it just says "self-employed")
- **Single-member LLCs** — by default, an SMLLC is a "disregarded entity":
  its activity flows onto your personal 1040 via Schedule C. (Your LLC can
  *elect* corporate taxation instead — that's a conversation with a pro and
  the LLC handbook, not this chapter.)
- Gig and freelance workers, resellers, YouTubers, Etsy/KDP sellers... anyone
  whose business income isn't payroll.

## Walking the form's shape (not every line, just the terrain)

Keep the actual form open beside you the first time:
[irs.gov — Schedule C PDF](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-schedule-c-form-1040).

### The top block — *who you are*

Name, your business's name (that's where your LLC name goes), address,
**EIN** if you have one (LLCs usually should — see the LLC handbook), and
your **business code** — a six-digit number describing your industry. Authors
usually land in a code for independent writers/publishers; resellers in a
retail code. There's an instruction booklet that lists them all; your tax
software can also just look it up for you.

### Section I — *money in (gross receipts)*

Everything your business received before subtracting anything. For authors,
that's the big soup: royalties, direct book sales, audiobook revenue,
affiliate income if it belongs to the business, ad revenue. (Chapter 02 covers
how to organize that soup.)

Line features worth knowing:

- **Returns and allowances** — refunds you issued customers.
- **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** — more below; it's important for *selling
  physical stuff*, less so for pure royalties.

### Section II — *money out (expenses)*

This is where Chapter 03's list plugs in. Advertising, supplies, software
subscriptions, a home office, professional services, and more. Each line is a
category; fill only the ones that apply to you. Blank lines are fine.

### The bottom sections — *your car, your house, and your other jobs*

- **Vehicle expenses:** driving for business reasons (book fair, picking up
  inventory, delivering). You choose per-vehicle standard mileage or actual
  expenses — the current rates are published annually on IRS.gov, so check
  rather than remembering a number from social media.
- **Home office:** if a space is *regularly and exclusively* used for the
  business, you may deduct a slice of rent/mortgage interest/utilities. It
  does **not** trigger audits by existing; it just needs to be real and
  documented. Photos + a simple floor plan are your friends.
- The form also asks about **wages you paid others** — most solo beginners
  leave this empty.

### Then — *self-employment tax*

Schedule C's profit also feeds **Schedule SE**, which computes
**self-employment (SE) tax** — roughly "you're both employee and employer, so
you pay both halves of Social Security/Medicare." It surprises every beginner:
a dollar of profit is taxed *more* than a dollar of W-2 wages for FICA
purposes. Nobody tells you this at the bookstore signing.
**SE tax is exactly why quarterly estimates matter** — see Chapter 04.

Optional (and pro-level): some taxpayers may deduct one employer-half of SE tax
above the line, and there's a Qualified Business Income (QBI) concept that can
reduce a slice of pass-through profit. Both have big if-branches. Learn the
names now; let your tax professional handle the if-branches.

### One more wire: the 1099s and what they *don't* mean

- Platforms (Amazon/KDP, IngramSpark, etc.) send a **1099-NEC or 1099-MISC**
  when they cross reporting thresholds — commonly $1,000+ in many recent years,
  but the threshold has moved around, so treat "no 1099" as *not* meaning
  "not taxable". **Taxable is not the same as "someone sent paperwork about
  it."** You report all of your profit whether or not a 1099 arrived.
- The IRS gets copies of your 1099s. Cross-check them against your records
  each January; platforms make mistakes, and you may need to fix or annotate.

## Small glossary in human words

| Word | Means |
|---|---|
| Profit / Net | Money in minus money out, per Schedule C's math |
| Gross receipts | All money in before anything is subtracted |
| COGS | The direct cost of the thing you sold (incl. printing, inventory) |
| EIN | A tax ID number for your business, free from the IRS |
| Filing schedule | A single "tax year" in which you honestly earned/spent |
| Estimated taxes | Pre-paying during the year (Chapter 04) |

## Call a pro when...

- You're choosing between sole prop, LLC, or S-corp election — their calendar
  and cost tradeoffs are real and person-specific.
- You ran payroll, hired family, or took owner draws and don't know how those
  interact with Schedule C.
- Two of your 1099s disagree with your books and you can't tell who's right.
- Your profit crosses into "oh this is real money" territory and you want a
  CPA or EA to sanity-check the first year you'll owe.
