# 05 — Receipts Without Panic

*The system for people whose filing style is "a pile exists somewhere and I
sense it." Designed low-energy, ADHD-friendly, and forgiving of imperfect
months.*

## Why receipts exist, on a friend-level

A receipt is not a legal talisman. It's a **tiny piece of evidence** that says:
"This money left, on this date, for this thing." Business expense *records*
should let you (or your pro) reconstruct the year from facts, not memory.
Digital photos of receipts are generally fine as records — the point is
readability and retrievability, not paper purity. (General IRS guidance
supports electronic records; your pro can confirm specifics for your setup.)

## The three-bucket system (about 90 seconds per expense)

Whenever money leaves the business:

1. **Capture** — photo or PDF into one inbox (a folder on your phone/desktop
   or a note app you already use). One, single inbox. Not six.
2. **De-minimis sort** (weekly, 5 minutes, cereal-on-the-couch effort):
   glance at each item; if it's obviously *personal* (your kid's shoes), it
   moves out; if it's obviously *business* (book conference ticket), it gets
   a quick filename like `2026-03-14_bookconf_ticket.png`.
3. **Log the row** (monthly, as part of Ch. 02's ritual): date, vendor, what,
   why, amount, category, filename.

That's it. Future-you (or your pro, or an auditor) can trace every dollar.

## Rules of thumb that keep this human

- **One inbox, one spreadsheet, one tax account.** System-scatter is the
  #1 killer of record keeping, not laziness.
- **Name files `YYYY-MM-DD_what`** — sortable, obvious, scannable.
- **A "why" column beats a perfect memory.** Six words now beats a 30-minute
  archaeology in March.
- **The business account is your friend.** If the money came from the
  business account, a bank statement line often corroborates the receipt —
  two independent records instead of one. Commingling is what makes receipts
  look like fiction.
- **Lost a receipt?** Not fatal. A bank/credit-card statement line, a vendor
  email invoice, or an order-history screenshot can form a reasonable record.
  Don't spiral. Document what you have.

## The monthly 10-minute ritual (full script)

Sunday evening (or whenever your brain says "oh right, money"):

1. **Platforms** (Ch. 02): last month's gross / fees / net per platform. One
   row each on the income tab.
2. **Expenses**: every receipt in the capture inbox gets a row on the expense
   tab. Inbox should hit *zero*.
3. **Transfer**: your tax slice into the tax account. Look at the number;
   say a small word of gratitude to past-you.
4. **Glance report**: income minus expenses this month. Story in one number.

If a month got away from you: run steps backwards. Reconstruct-from-statement
is allowed; it's how every small business actually survives.

## Digital tools (generic categories, not endorsements)

- **Camera capture apps** that auto-crop receipts and PDF them
- **Bookkeeping apps** that connect the business bank account (then you
  categorize and *reconcile* — still glance at platform statements, per Ch. 02)
- **A plain folder tree**, honestly, works fine:

```
books/2026/
  income-statements/
  receipts/
  1099s/
  estimates/
```

## What to keep and for how long

Basic guidance that's been stable: keep returns typically ~3 years (longer in
some cases — e.g., certain issues reach 6 years, and there are special cases;
a tax professional can give you a real retention answer). Practical, calm
choose: **keep 7 years** of expense records and tax paperwork. Storage is
cheap; anxiety is not. (Also confirm with a pro for anything involving
property you later sell — those records have longer useful lives.)

## Celebration clause

Every month this ritual happens is a win, whether income was $2,000 or $20.
Compounding calm is the actual financial strategy of small business. This
chapter is the most important one in the book even though it has the fewest
IRS numbers, and that is not an accident.
